# Angola: offline / quiet-industries pass

**Budget used:** 20 of 20 WebSearch calls. WebFetch was not used, so every fact comes from search-result summaries and their linked pages. Anything I could not confirm from a primary source is marked "unverified".

**Bottom line:** Angola's quiet industries are mostly **informal and are being policed by bans, not by registers**. In January 2026 scrap dealers were banned outright. Street vendors, moto-taxis and candongueiros are handled through cards and crackdowns. Cemeteries have no law at all. Only a few quiet industries have a recurring paper obligation that a small business would pay to offload. The three below are weak (3–4/10). None is a build recommendation. A non-local solo founder would also struggle in all three because of Portuguese, kwanza collection and the need for in-person sales in Luanda.

Existing country report: `research/countries/angola.md` (e-invoice reconciliation for accountants, 4/10). That opportunity is not repeated here.

## 2. Strongest opportunities (all weak)

### Opportunity: Done-for-you INSS formalisation for household employers (domestic workers)

**Industry:**
Households as employers (domestic workers, nannies, drivers, guards employed by a household)

**Buyer:**
Upper-middle-income and expatriate households in Luanda (Talatona, Miramar, Alvalade) that employ 1–3 domestic staff. A secondary buyer is the HR department of companies that pay household staff for expatriate executives as a benefit (unverified practice).

**Trigger / Why now:**
In September 2025 Standard Bank Angola and INSS launched "Domésticos Protegidos", which put the formalisation of domestic work on the public agenda. Labour-law follow-on diplomas at the end of 2025 tightened enforcement and employer accountability in general (Miranda Law Firm, Dec 2025–Mar 2026 bulletin). It is unverified whether these specifically target household employers.

**Current workflow:**
1. The employer registers as a domestic employer and registers the worker with INSS under DP 155/16, in person or through the INSS channels.
2. The employer works out the salary and the 5% + 3% (or 11%) contribution each month.
3. The employer pays through a RUPE reference at a bank, ATM or Multicaixa Express (inferred) and keeps receipts.
4. Payslips (recibos) are handwritten or not issued at all.
5. In practice, about 98% skip all of this (13,160 registered out of about 722,000).

**Pain:**
The pain is weak and latent. The cost of non-compliance is mostly borne by the worker (no pension or maternity cover), not by the employer, and I found no evidence of fines on household employers. The pain for the employer appears at termination (severance disputes) and at visa/residency checks for expatriate families (unverified).

**Existing solutions:**
- Standard Bank Angola "Domésticos Protegidos": a digital platform for employer and worker registration, account opening, monthly auto-debit of contributions and direct RUPE transfer and reporting to INSS. It is free or bank-subsidised (pricing unverified).
- Direct INSS counter registration and RUPE payment.
- Labour lawyers and accountants on an ad hoc basis.
- Global EOR/payroll vendors do not serve households.

**Offline evidence:**
A 1.8% registration rate. A bank had to build its own channel. Contributions are paid through RUPE references rather than through a payroll file.

**Offline channel:**
Expatriate HR managers at oil service companies and embassies (household-staff benefit packages), relocation agencies in Luanda, and domestic-staff placement agencies (agências de emprego doméstico, count unverified). It could also be offered as a reseller layer on top of SBA's platform.

**Market count:**
About 722,000 domestic workers (INE via Expansão). The addressable paying segment (expatriate and affluent households) is an estimate of a few thousand households.

**The gap:**
SBA's platform handles contributions and reporting. Its coverage of the employment contract, payslips, leave and severance calculation (DP 155/16 rules) and termination paperwork is unverified. That is the possible gap, but it is thin.

**Possible product:**
A "household employer kit": a contract template under DP 155/16, monthly payslip, contribution calculator, RUPE reminder, and a severance calculation at exit. It would be sold as a done-for-you service to expatriate households through relocation and HR partners.

**MVP:**
A WhatsApp-plus-PDF service: monthly payslip and contribution statement per household, with a human doing the INSS registration in person.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
Estimate: USD 10–20 per household per month for expatriate households, paid by the employer company. Local households would likely pay close to nothing.

**How to find first customers:**
Relocation agencies, international schools' parent networks, and oil and gas HR departments in Luanda. Placement agencies that already handle domestic staff.

**Risks:**
SBA (a bank with INSS backing) is the default and is free. Enforcement on households is weak. Payment in kwanza. The service needs someone local to visit INSS counters.

**Kill condition:**
Kill the idea if SBA's platform already issues payslips and severance calculations, or if expatriate HR departments already outsource this to their payroll provider.

**Willingness to pay / founder access:** Buyers would pay only for a done-for-you service, not for software. A non-local solo founder could not run this without a local partner.

**Score:** 3/10

### Opportunity: Controlled-medicine register and quarterly ARMED maps for independent pharmacies

**Industry:**
Independent pharmacies (farmácias) and drugstores

**Buyer:**
The owner or technical director (director técnico) of an independent pharmacy or a small chain (2–10 outlets)

**Trigger / Why now:**
The licensing regime was rebuilt under DP 41/24 (RLEAF, Jan 2024), with inspections within 30 days and a closure process for illegal outlets. ARMED is digitising (SIREMA medicine registration, 2024) and is working to reach WHO Maturity Level 3 by 2027 under its institutional plan. A new fee regime for ARMED followed (Decreto Executivo Conjunto 1/25). It is unverified whether ARMED will move the narcotic and psychotropic maps to an electronic format.

**Current workflow:**
1. The pharmacy records every entry and exit of narcotic or psychotropic medicines in a register (format unverified, likely a paper book).
2. Each quarter, it fills in the regulator-approved "mapas" of narcotics and psychotropics.
3. It submits them to the regulator within 20 days of the quarter end (Lei 4/99 framework, article unverified).
4. It keeps prescriptions to match exits against them for inspections.

**Pain:**
The evidence is indirect: a recurring mandatory filing, active inspections and closures, and widespread counterfeit medicines (VerAngola cites "half the medicines in circulation are counterfeit", 2018). I found no direct complaints about the maps.

**Existing solutions:**
- A paper register and regulator form models.
- Pharmacy POS/ERP software: Primavera/Cegid with a pharmacy vertical, and local Angolan pharmacy software. Whether these produce the ARMED maps is unverified. I did not have the budget to check.
- Distributors that supply stock-management tools (unverified).
- Accountants and pharmaceutical consultants who handle licensing files.

**Offline evidence:**
Regulator-approved paper map models, a regulator that is still digitising, and no pharmacy-compliance software listings found.

**Offline channel:**
The Ordem dos Farmacêuticos de Angola (existence and directory unverified), the major medicine distributors that visit every pharmacy weekly, and the ARMED public register of licensed establishments used for phone or WhatsApp outreach.

**Market count:**
3,692 licensed establishments in the ARMED register dated 8 Jul 2026, via a secondary source (meddeviceguide.com). The number of retail pharmacies within that is unknown (estimate: 1,500–2,500).

**The gap:**
A pharmacy that already has POS software may still keep the controlled-drug book by hand and compile quarterly maps manually. If so, the gap is generating the maps automatically from dispensing records.

**Possible product:**
A light controlled-medicine register (scan or enter the prescription and dispense against it) that outputs the quarterly ARMED narcotic and psychotropic maps in the approved model, plus a licence-renewal and inspection checklist under RLEAF.

**MVP:**
A spreadsheet-import tool: upload the POS sales export, filter controlled substances against a list, and print the quarterly map in the ARMED format.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
Estimate: USD 15–30 per pharmacy per month. It is more realistic as an add-on sold through a POS vendor or distributor.

**How to find first customers:**
The ARMED establishment register, distributor sales representatives, and the pharmacists' professional body.

**Risks:**
Controlled-drug dispensing volumes in small Angolan pharmacies may be tiny, which makes the map trivial. ARMED may build an e-submission into its own system. POS vendors could add it cheaply.

**Kill condition:**
Kill the idea if interviews show the quarterly map takes less than an hour per quarter, or if the main Angolan pharmacy POS systems already produce it.

**Willingness to pay / founder access:** Software at a low price is possible, but the realistic route is as a distributor or POS add-on. It needs Portuguese and a local partner, so it is not suitable for a non-local solo founder.

**Score:** 4/10 (low confidence)

### Opportunity: Lesson-log and inspection-readiness file for driving schools

**Industry:**
Driving schools (escolas de condução)

**Buyer:**
The owner or director of a driving school

**Trigger / Why now:**
The DTSER (traffic police) says about half of the 379 active schools risk closure for failing to meet requirements (vehicles, facilities, training standards). The driving-teaching regulation sets 32 practical and 28 theory lessons for category B, restricted lesson hours (Mon–Thu, 10:00–17:00 and 22:00–24:00), and at least 3 vehicles with one per category. In 2026 the government also announced a road-safety education push.

**Current workflow (inferred, unverified):**
1. A student enrols and the school opens a paper file.
2. The instructor records each lesson on paper.
3. The school compiles the file to present the candidate for the DTSER theory and practical exams.
4. For inspections, the school shows its alvará, vehicle documents and instructor credentials.

**Pain:**
The closure threat is documented. There is no evidence about lesson logging specifically.

**Existing solutions:**
Paper files and generic school-management or spreadsheet tools. Portuguese driving-school software (for example, products sold to Portuguese schools) is unverified for Angola. Exam-prep apps (open-exam-prep) cover candidates, not schools.

**Offline evidence:**
Police-run oversight, no Angolan driving-school software found, and exams administered by the Polícia Nacional.

**Offline channel:**
The DTSER list of licensed schools (obtained through the police directorate, availability unverified), a driving-school association (not found), and in-person visits in Luanda.

**Market count:**
379 schools (DTSER via Expansão).

**The gap:**
Proof that each candidate completed the 32 lessons within the allowed hours, and a ready inspection file. This would only matter if DTSER actually checks lesson logs (unverified).

**Possible product:**
A mobile lesson log with time and GPS stamps per instructor and student, and a printable candidate file and inspection dossier.

**MVP:**
An instructor app plus a PDF candidate file.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
Estimate: USD 30–60 per school per month.

**How to find first customers:**
Visit schools in Luanda, and go through the DTSER Department of Drivers.

**Risks:**
379 schools at about USD 40 per month is a ceiling of about USD 180k a year. Enforcement may result in closures rather than better paperwork.

**Kill condition:**
Kill the idea if DTSER does not inspect lesson records, or if schools are closed for reasons software can't fix (vehicles, premises).

**Willingness to pay / founder access:** Some would pay for software, but the market is too small to justify it. It needs local sales.

**Score:** 3/10

## 3. Rejected

- **Scrap-metal origin traceability (Decreto Executivo 7/26).** This was the strongest trigger found: on 9 Jan 2026 all scrap and weighing licences were revoked, and foundries and steel mills must document the lawful origin of their raw material or face infraction proceedings. Rejected because the small-dealer side is now illegal and the obliged buyers are a handful of steel mills (an enterprise sale). The scrap association ASUA is lobbying for an operator cadastro. If the government reopens the sector with a register, this becomes worth a fresh look.
- **Moto-taxi and candongueiro compliance.** The riders and drivers have almost no ability to pay. Licensing is municipal and disorganised, and the inspection non-compliance rate is about 97%. The only buyer would be government (procurement).
- **Street vendors and markets.** The state already issues digital vendor cards via the Citizen Portal (DP 111/24), and the vendors have no money.
- **Kinguilas (street money changers).** The activity is banned, not regulated.
- **Artisanal fisheries.** About 8,000 unlicensed boats. Licensing is a state quota tool (DP 57/25), and the fishers can't pay.
- **Funeral agencies and cemeteries.** There is no regulating law, so no forcing function. Only crematoria are regulated (DP 178/22).
- **Livestock traders, abattoirs and well drillers.** I found no SME-side recurring filing within budget. ANIESA's centralisation (DP 142/26, Aug 2026) is one to watch for abattoirs and butchers.

## 4. Method notes

- What worked: Portuguese regulator-first queries naming the decree ("Decreto Executivo 7/26", "DP 41/24", "DP 155/16") and the trade's local name (sucateiros, candongueiros, zungueiras, kinguilas). The business weekly **Expansão** (expansao.co.ao) and **VerAngola** were the best sources for counts and enforcement. **lex.ao** and **angolex.com** host the gazette texts, and the Miranda and PLMJ law-firm notes summarise new decrees.
- What didn't: livestock movement permits, well-drilling licences, cemeteries and DTSER exam workflow queries returned nothing usable. Official portals (ARMED, INSS, INRH) do not surface in search, and WebFetch was not allowed.
- Pattern: Angola's typical regulatory response to informal quiet industries is **prohibition or crackdown**, not a register with recurring filings. That removes the recurring-workflow pattern the brief looks for. The regulated quiet segments that do file (pharmacies, driving schools) are small, a few hundred to a few thousand operators.
