# Botswana: Offline (Quiet) Industries Pass

**Research date:** 2026-10-05 · **Searches used:** 20 of 20 · **Existing report:** `research/countries/botswana.md` (BURS e-invoicing/EFD, DNFBP AML kit and tender packs are not repeated here)

**Bottom line:** Botswana's quiet industries are regulated, but mostly by **one central government counter or council office**, often for free. That leaves little for a software vendor to "route" between authorities. Most obligations are licence renewals (annual, sometimes 5-yearly) rather than recurring per-job filings. There are also few operators (about 2.5M people), and the 2024 minimum-wage order and the 2022 Societies Act are the only fresh triggers that touch small operators. No idea scored above 4/10. The two least-bad leads are a **private-security compliance pack** and a **done-for-you Societies Act annual-return service for burial societies and churches**. Both are better as a local service business than as indie SaaS, and both need a local founder or partner.

## 2. Strongest opportunities

### Opportunity: Licence-renewal and wage-compliance pack for citizen-owned security companies

**Industry:**
Private security guard services (guarding, cash-in-transit excluded).

**Buyer:**
Owner-director of a small or medium citizen-owned security company (about 20–300 guards), who often does the admin personally or with one clerk.

**Trigger / Why now:**
- The Private Security Services Act replaced the old guard-companies law. Licences **expire annually**, and every guard needs a Security Guard Certificate from the Private Security Services Licensing Board.
- The Daily News reports that security companies that fail to comply with a wage order **would have their licences cancelled**. This ties labour compliance directly to the right to trade.
- The minimum wage for guards is P9.06/hour under the Regulation of Minimum Wages Order, S.I. 7 of 2024. It is unchanged in 2025–2026, so this is not a fresh trigger.
- The trigger is moderate: the obligation is established, not new.

**Current workflow:**
1. The owner keeps guard files on paper: Omang copy, police clearance, training certificate and Security Guard Certificate.
2. Shift rosters are kept in Excel or exercise books. Hours are summed by hand to check that pay is at least P9.06/hour, including overtime.
3. Every year the owner reassembles the licence-renewal pack (CIPA extract, tax clearance, guard list and certificates) and takes it to the Board.
4. Client tenders (government, parastatals, mines) ask for the same documents again.

**Pain:**
Losing the licence ends the business, and labour-law non-adherence in the sector has been studied academically (University of Limpopo thesis "An assessment of private security sector adherence to Botswana employment law"). No direct operator complaints were found online, which is expected for a quiet industry. The size of the pain is **unverified**.

**Existing solutions:**
- Paper files and Excel.
- Generic payroll (Sage VIP / Sage Payroll Professional through local partners, PaySpace), which handles pay but not guard-certificate expiry or Board renewal packs.
- South African guard-management/roster software. It is likely sold into Botswana, but no specific vendor was verified in this session.
- Local accountants and "tender runners" who assemble documents.

**Offline evidence:**
The Board's process is a counter application listed on gov.bw with no online portal. Guard certificates are individual paper documents. No Botswana-specific security-compliance software listing was found.

**Offline channel:**
- Security company names must contain the word "SECURITY" under CIPA rules, so a CIPA name search gives a prospect list for phone and WhatsApp outreach.
- The Private Security Services Licensing Board's licensee list could be requested (its availability is unverified).
- Security-industry employers' associations (none verified by name).
- Payroll bureaus that already serve guard companies.

**Market count:**
Unknown. No public count of licensed companies was found. Several hundred firms is an **estimate**.

**The gap:**
Nothing joins guard certificates, roster hours, minimum-wage checks and the annual Board renewal pack in one record.

**Possible product:**
A guard register: one record per guard with certificate expiry alerts, roster import, an automatic check that hours × rate meets the wage order, and one-click generation of the Board renewal pack and client tender pack.

**MVP:**
A spreadsheet-import guard register, expiry reminders by WhatsApp, a wage-order check report, and a PDF renewal pack.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
P10–15 per guard per month (estimate). For a 100-guard firm that is about P1,000–1,500/month. Owners are likelier to pay for a done-for-you renewal service (P2,000–5,000 per renewal, estimate) than for software.

**How to find first customers:**
CIPA name search on "SECURITY", the Board's licensee list, payroll bureaus, and visits to firms in Gaborone and Francistown.

**Risks:**
- The Board could build an online portal.
- Global or South African guard-tour/roster SaaS may already cover the operational side.
- Owners may tolerate wage non-compliance instead of paying to prove compliance.
- Data Protection Act 2024 transfer rules apply to guard ID data.

**Kill condition:**
Interviews show the Board rarely cancels licences and renewals take an owner less than a day. Or a South African guard-management product already offers Botswana certificate tracking.

**Willingness to pay:**
Mostly service, with software as a back-office tool.

**Founder access:**
Needs a local. The buyers are citizen-only firms reached in person, and the Board counter is in Botswana.

**Score:** 4/10

### Opportunity: Done-for-you Societies Act annual returns for burial societies and churches

**Industry:**
Burial societies, churches, social and sporting clubs, and community associations registered under the Societies Act 2022.

**Buyer:**
The secretary or treasurer of a registered society: volunteers, often older. Accounting firms that keep society books are a secondary buyer.

**Trigger / Why now:**
- The Societies Act 2022 required **re-registration on a new electronic system** (24 Nov 2023 – 24 Feb 2024).
- Afterwards, **5,796 organisations were struck off** and only 2,240 were re-registered, including burial societies and churches.
- Surviving societies must file an annual return (P50) every year, and the Registrar can cancel registration for failing to provide financial information.
- The Registrar has also issued public advice to churches (Daily News).

**Current workflow:**
1. The treasurer keeps a cash book and contribution register on paper or in a WhatsApp group.
2. Once a year someone compiles the members, office-bearers and finances, and files the return on the Registrar's electronic system (or asks someone who can).
3. Burial payouts and member contributions are reconciled by hand at the AGM.

**Pain:**
The mass strike-off proves that many societies failed even the free re-registration. Losing registration can mean losing the bank account and legal standing. The pain is real, but each society files only once a year.

**Existing solutions:**
- The Registrar's own electronic system.
- Local accountants and bookkeepers.
- Excel and exercise books.
- Mobile-money/bank group accounts (unverified for burial-society features).

**Offline evidence:**
The Registrar was fully manual until 2023. Societies are volunteer-run, and the strike-off figures show members failed to use the online system.

**Offline channel:**
- District Registrar of Societies offices.
- Church councils and denominational bodies.
- Kgotla meetings.
- Banks holding society accounts.

**Market count:**
2,240 re-registered societies (The Gazette). The number that are burial societies is unknown.

**The gap:**
Small societies need someone to turn a contribution register into a compliant annual return each year, and to keep the member and payout ledger in between.

**Possible product:**
A contribution and payout ledger for burial societies that also generates the annual return pack, sold with an "we file it for you" service.

**MVP:**
WhatsApp-friendly contribution logging plus an annual-return PDF and data sheet.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
P50–150/month per society, or P300–600 per filing as a service (estimate). Ability to pay is low.

**How to find first customers:**
Registrar offices, church federations, and banks' society-account desks.

**Risks:**
- Very low willingness to pay.
- Burial societies may be few compared with churches and clubs.
- The Registrar may simplify returns further.

**Kill condition:**
Interviews show the annual return is a 20-minute form, or treasurers will not pay more than P50 a year.

**Willingness to pay:**
Service only.

**Founder access:**
Needs a local, with Setswana-language trust-based selling.

**Score:** 3/10

## 3. Rejected

- **Livestock trader, agent and feedlot permits during the 2026 FMD crisis.** The trigger is real: suspected FMD from 28 Jan 2026, all movement permits revoked, the EU withdrew fresh-beef authorisation on 21 Apr 2026, and internal movement eased on 5 May 2026. But permits and BAITS (registration, transfers, treatments, deaths) are free government systems, run through DVS offices and **BAITS cafés/agents**. The cafés are the substitute and already the offline channel. Buyer licences fall under Cap 36:01, and the number of licensees is unknown. This is a government or donor project, not indie SaaS.
- **Domestic-worker payroll.** The only obligation is the P1,500 minimum wage and a written contract. There is no social security or pension fund, so there is nothing to file each month.
- **Scrap metal registers.** The register exists under council bye-laws, but the dealer count is tiny and there is no enforcement evidence.
- **Liquor licensing.** The government's own amendment bill proposes online licensing.
- **Taxi/combi permits, hawkers, funeral parlours, borehole drillers and hunting outfitters.** Each is a single counter, an annual or one-off task, few operators or no ability to pay (see table).
- **Export abattoirs.** About 3–4 buyers, all enterprise-grade.

## 4. Method notes

- **Worked:** gov.bw service pages (`gov.bw/<sector>/<licence>`). They describe every licence, its counter, fee and documents, and confirm the workflow is offline. Daily News (dailynews.gov.bw) gives enforcement news such as the societies strike-off and the FMD permit revocations. botswanalaws.com shows the council bye-laws.
- **Did not work:** counts. No licensing body publishes a register of licensees with numbers online. Searching for "second-hand goods" returned only South African law. Local-language (Setswana) queries were not attempted because regulators publish in English.
- **Structural finding:** Botswana's quiet-industry obligations converge on one central counter per industry, so the "one job, many receiving authorities" pattern from the brief is largely absent. The exception is council bye-laws (scrap, hawkers), which are fragmented across councils but cover industries with no money.
