# Malawi: Offline (Quiet) Industries Pass

Research date: 2026-10-05. Budget: 20 WebSearch calls, all used. WebFetch was not used, so everything comes from search-result summaries. Anything I could not confirm is marked "unverified" or "estimate".

**Context from the country report (`research/countries/malawi.md`).** Malawi is a small, cash-poor market in a deep foreign-exchange crisis. Any product would have to be billed in kwacha (MWK), usually through a local partner. That constraint hits quiet industries even harder than the formal SMEs covered in the first pass. Most operators here are informal, have very little cash, and deal with regulators in person at a counter or through their association.

**Honest headline:** none of the quiet industries in Malawi clears the bar for a software business. The regulatory triggers are real: the kabaza registration moratorium and crackdown, the PMRA fee regime of March 2026, and the suspension and vandalism crackdown on scrap metal. But the operators either can't pay, or the obligation is annual and handled at a counter. The two ideas written up below are the best of a weak set, and both score 3/10.

## 2. Strongest opportunities

### Opportunity: Kabaza association compliance register (member licence and registration tracker for associations and councils)

**Industry:**
Motorcycle-taxi (kabaza) transport.

**Buyer:**
Kabaza associations and the national coalition (Macokasa), plus district or city councils that sign MoUs with the associations. The buyer is not the individual rider, who cannot pay.

**Trigger / Why now:**
- The DRTSS registration amnesty (reduced fees of K56,000 for 0–100cc and K77,000 for 101–250cc) ran from March 2025. It was extended to December 2025, then extended again for 6 months from 1 April 2026. That extension should have ended around 30 September 2026, about now (inferred, not confirmed).
- Police, councils and associations began joint enforcement in August 2026, with impoundments and clashes in Blantyre and Limbe.
- Macokasa is pushing for "total compliance", lower fees, and for kabaza to be organised under cooperatives.

**Current workflow:**
1. The rider registers the motorcycle at a DRTSS counter, pays the fee, and gets a driving licence separately.
2. The rider buys a council licence or permit and joins a local association or stage committee.
3. Associations keep member lists on paper or in WhatsApp groups. During crackdowns they negotiate with police and councils over which riders are compliant.
4. Police and councils check documents at the roadside and impound non-compliant bikes.

**Pain:**
- Impoundments and crackdowns (814 kabaza crashes, 162 deaths and 652 injuries in 2025, per police).
- The registration cost of about K200,000 outside the amnesty is unaffordable for many riders.
- Associations have no reliable way to prove which members are compliant when councils sign MoUs.

**Existing solutions:**
- The DRTSS Malawi Traffic Information System (MalTIS), which is government-run and being upgraded.
- Paper membership books, WhatsApp groups, and association ID cards.
- Microfinance lenders and asset financiers who finance bikes and may track paperwork (unverified).

**Offline evidence:**
Registration and licensing happen at DRTSS and council counters. Enforcement is physical. The associations are informal stage-based groups. I found no software listings.

**Offline channel:**
Through Macokasa and its district MoUs (for example, the Mchinji District Council MoU), or through council transport offices. A local partner is essential to reach the first 10 associations.

**Market count:**
About 12,000 bikes registered during the 2025 amnesty (DRTSS). The total fleet and the number of associations are unverified.

**The gap:**
No shared, checkable register links rider, bike registration, licence expiry and association membership, for councils to use in enforcement and for lenders or insurers.

**Possible product:**
An association-run member register: rider ID, plate number, DRTSS registration and licence expiry, council permit and insurance. It would send SMS expiry reminders and issue a QR card that councils and police can scan.

**MVP:**
A web or Android register for one association in one city, with SMS reminders and a printable QR member card.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
About MWK 500–1,000 per member per month, collected through association dues (estimate). Alternatively, a flat annual fee paid by a council, NGO or road-safety donor.

**How to find first customers:**
Macokasa leadership, district council MoU signatories, and road-safety programmes run by donors.

**Risks:**
- Riders have extremely low income, and association dues are irregular.
- Councils have no budget.
- DRTSS may add a registry feature of its own.
- The idea is politically sensitive during crackdowns.
- A non-local founder could not sell this; it needs a local operator.

**Kill condition:**
Kill the idea if associations won't charge members for it, or if no council or donor will pay within 3 interviews.

**Willingness to pay / founder access:**
Buyers would pay, if at all, for a done-for-you service funded by a donor or council, not for software. This is not feasible for a non-local solo founder.

**Score:** 3/10

### Opportunity: Medicine-store compliance and expiry register (PMRA inspection-ready)

**Industry:**
Medicine stores and small retail pharmacies.

**Buyer:**
The owner of a licensed medicine store, or the supervising pharmacist who oversees several stores.

**Trigger / Why now:**
- PMRA published a new fee structure effective 27 March 2026. It sets the medicine store retention fee at K200,000 and the retail pharmacy fee at K500,000, plus re-inspection fees for premises that failed or were closed.
- Penalties apply if retention is not paid by 31 March.
- PMRA publishes enforcement actions against pharmacy personnel (May 2025).
- The PMRA Act sets fines of up to K20m for unlicensed trade.

**Current workflow:**
1. Keep paper stock and expiry records and a sales or dispensing book (exact register requirements unverified).
2. Pay the annual retention fee at PMRA or through the bank.
3. Prepare for inspection by hand. Stores fail inspections for expired stock or for stocking medicines beyond the medicine-store category.
4. At an exempt-supply level, also comply with MRA EIS invoicing (covered in the country report).

**Pain:**
- Closures, such as 35 drug stores in Lilongwe for keeping medicines beyond their mandate and past expiry.
- Arrests for unlicensed premises.
- Infrequent supervision means stores find out they are non-compliant at the inspection itself.

**Existing solutions:**
- Paper ledgers sold by stationers.
- Generic pharmacy POS systems sold by local software houses and resellers (specific vendors unverified).
- Supervising pharmacists' own visits.
- PMRA guidance and checklists.

**Offline evidence:**
PMRA licensing is handled at its office (Area 5, Lilongwe). Enforcement is by physical inspection and police raids. I found no Malawi-specific SaaS for medicine stores.

**Offline channel:**
- Wholesale pharmacies (82 per the old count) that supply the stores.
- The Pharmaceutical Society of Malawi (not verified in this pass).
- Supervising pharmacists, each of whom oversees several stores.

**Market count:**
754 medicine stores and 87 retail pharmacies (old PMPB registrar figure, year unverified).

**The gap:**
An expiry and "allowed-category" checker matched to the medicine-store schedule, producing an inspection-ready stock report. Generic POS systems do not encode which medicines a medicine-store licence allows.

**Possible product:**
An Android stock register that flags expiring stock and items outside the store's licence category. It would produce a monthly inspection pack and remind the owner of the 31 March retention deadline.

**MVP:**
Barcode or manual stock entry, an expiry alert, a hard-coded medicine-store schedule, and a printable report.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
About MWK 15,000–30,000 per month (about US$9–17, estimate). This is plausible only when sold through a wholesaler bundle.

**How to find first customers:**
Wholesale pharmacies' customer lists, supervising pharmacists, and PMRA's licensed-premises lists, if they are published (unverified).

**Risks:**
- Fewer than 1,000 buyers.
- The product is close to generic pharmacy POS.
- The annual obligation is weak, and the daily part (stock control) is optional in law (unverified).
- Forex constraints apply.
- It needs a local partner.

**Kill condition:**
Kill the idea if PMRA does not require a specific register or category control that stores struggle with, or if wholesalers already bundle free stock software.

**Willingness to pay / founder access:**
Owners might pay a small amount for software bundled with their wholesale supply. A non-local founder would need a wholesaler partner.

**Score:** 3/10

## 3. Rejected

- **Scrap-metal dealer police register.** The Second-hand and Scrap Metal Dealers Act requires registration with the Commissioner of Police, and police run a clearance service (police.gov.mw/services/scrap-metal-clearance). But licence issuance was suspended and exports banned in 2023, the Central Region dealers' association is lobbying to lift this, and enforcement targets vandalism of utility and railway infrastructure (ESCOM, CEAR). There is no stable legal customer base and the count is unknown.
- **Minibus CoF and permits (MOAM).** The pain comes from DRTSS system outages, not operator paperwork. The substitute is queuing.
- **AIP agro-dealers.** There is a single government payer, and payments are stopped or delayed (a K50bn debt). The pain is political and financial, not a workflow problem.
- **Produce or maize buyer licences.** Licensing is annual and seasonal, and the export bans are erratic.
- **Livestock movement permits.** These are paper permits issued by district veterinary officers. They are low value and nobody would pay for software.
- **Household employers.** Domestic workers' minimum wage is MK72,800/month, but enforcement and filing are effectively absent.
- **Borehole drillers (NWRA).** There are few drillers, the market is driven by NGO tenders, and licensing is not recurring per job.
- **Private schools (ISAMA, 2,000+ members).** The recurring work is generic school administration, which is crowded. MANEB has already digitised fee payment.
- **Small food processors (MBS).** Certification is voluntary, and SMEDI and MBS already subsidise and support it.

## 4. Method notes

- What worked: searching the Nation (mwnation.com), MBC and the government news agency MANA (manaonline) news archives for "<industry> crackdown / licence / register" surfaced enforcement and counts. The regulators' own sites (pmra.mw fee schedule PDFs, malawilii.org for Acts) gave the obligations.
- What didn't work: searches for registers and lists returned almost nothing. No public lists of licensed dealers, drillers or agro-dealers were indexed. Local-language (Chichewa) queries were not attempted, because official records are in English.
- Constraints: the 20-search budget limited count verification. Most market counts are unverified. The underlying limit is the market itself: quiet Malawian industries are informal and cash-poor, so even real triggers lead only to donor- or service-funded models.
