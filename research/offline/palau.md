# Palau: offline-industries pass

**Classification:** Microstate (population about 18,000, estimate). Search budget: 8 WebSearch calls, all 8 used. WebFetch not used.
**Bottom line:** Palau has quiet, regulated industries: foreign-worker employers, tour operators and guides, taxi jeeps, pig farmers, inshore fish buyers and food premises. But every one of them has buyer counts in the tens to low hundreds. Most obligations are filed on paper at a single national or Koror State counter, so there is no "one job, many receiving authorities" fragmentation. **None meets the brief's bar as a standalone product.** I record two weak leads below. They make sense only as modules of a regional Micronesia product (Palau, FSM, RMI, Guam, CNMI) or as a done-for-you service run by a local.

## 2. Strongest opportunities

Both are weak. They are recorded so the cross-country ranking can see they were considered.

### Opportunity: Foreign-worker permit calendar and renewal-pack service for Palau employers

**Industry:**
Employers of guest workers (construction, hotels, restaurants, retail, farms)

**Buyer:**
Owner or office manager of a Koror business with 5 or more foreign workers, or a local labour/recruitment agent filing for several employers

**Trigger / Why now:**
Statutory cap of 6,000 guest workers and the 25%-of-population limit. Since the cap is binding, a lapsed permit can mean losing the slot. Bangladesh applications were suspended on Dec 2, 2024. Over 100 expired permits were reported, and a task force was set up over discrepancies in the foreign-worker count. Enforcement attention is high. I found no 2025–2026 rule change that creates a new obligation.

**Current workflow:**
1. Employer posts the vacancy publicly for 30 days to show no qualified local is available.
2. Employer collects the worker's passport, contract, medical certificate, police clearance and qualifications.
3. Employer submits the paper form, or the electronic form by email to the Division of Labor, and pays fees, including a $100 fee in a recent measure (status unverified).
4. Employer tracks yearly renewal dates, usually in a spreadsheet or by memory, then repeats steps 2–3. A change of employer means a new permit.

**Pain:**
Expired permits (more than 100 in one report), count discrepancies that prompted a task force, abandonment cases and labour-abuse complaints. A lapsed permit risks penalties and losing a slot under the cap.

**Existing solutions:**
In-house admin staff, local recruitment/manpower agents, Excel, and the Division of Labor's own forms and email intake. International EOR firms (Multiplier, Pebl) publish Palau guides but serve foreign companies, not local SMEs.

**Offline evidence:**
Applications go in on paper or as email attachments to a palaunet.com address. No portal or software listing was found. The Division of Labor reaches employers through in-person employer workshops.

**Offline channel:**
Division of Labor employer workshops, the Palau Chamber of Commerce, the Pacific Islands SBDC Palau centre, and the local recruitment agents themselves as resellers.

**Market count:**
Up to 6,000 permits (statutory cap, Marianas Variety). I found no count of employers. My estimate is 200–500 employing businesses (unverified).

**The gap:**
No one tracks per-worker expiry dates, the vacancy-notice window and document validity (medical, police clearance) together, and no one assembles the renewal pack.

**Possible product:**
A renewal calendar per worker that produces a pre-filled Division of Labor form and a document checklist 60 days before expiry. It would be sold mainly as a done-for-you filing service by a local agent.

**MVP:**
Spreadsheet import of workers and expiry dates, plus SMS/email reminders and a PDF form filler.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
US$3–5 per worker per month as software (unlikely to be bought). As a service, a US$50–100 fee per renewal is more realistic.

**How to find first customers:**
Attendee lists from Division of Labor workshops, Chamber members, and ads in Island Times.

**Risks:**
Tiny market. The government could build its own system after the task force. Buyers want a person, not software. It needs a local presence. A non-local solo founder could not realistically sell this.

**Kill condition:**
The Division of Labor launches an online permit system, or fewer than 100 employers hold 5 or more foreign workers.

**Willingness to pay / founder access:**
Buyers would pay only for a done-for-you service. A local is required.

**Score:** 2/10

### Opportunity: Tour-operator credential tracker (BOT licence + Koror guide cards + work permits)

**Industry:**
Dive shops, Rock Islands tour and boat operators

**Buyer:**
Dive or tour shop manager in Koror

**Trigger / Why now:**
Tour operator licensing moved to the Bureau of Tourism (EO 391), and BOT has publicly urged operators to apply. Koror State has amended its Tour Guide Certification Regulations and published a "TGCP Transition" notice (dates unverified). Operators met in Koror to discuss compliance issues.

**Current workflow:**
1. Operator applies for the BOT Tour Operator's License with boat-ownership proof.
2. Each guide holds a Koror DCLE certification card, renewed yearly after a refresher course, plus current CPR/First Aid. Non-citizen guides also need a national work permit valid for guiding.
3. On each trip the operator must keep the dive guide-to-customer ratio at 1:6.
4. The manager tracks all of this by hand.

**Pain:**
A guide whose card has lapsed cannot work in the Rock Islands, which is a revenue issue. Compliance meetings suggest friction. I found no published fines or prosecutions.

**Existing solutions:**
Dive-shop booking software (generic international tools, not verified for Palau), spreadsheets, and the DCLE training office itself.

**Offline evidence:**
Credentials are issued through classroom courses and counters. KSG regulations and the guide manual are published as PDFs.

**Offline channel:**
DCLE refresher course sessions, BOT licensing meetings, and the Belau Tourism Association (existence and membership unverified).

**Market count:**
Unknown. My estimate is under 150 operators (unverified).

**The gap:**
No single view of operator licence, guide cards, CPR expiry and work-permit expiry, linked to trip rosters and the 1:6 ratio.

**Possible product:**
A credential and roster checker for dive and tour shops.

**MVP:**
A guide list with expiry dates, plus a daily roster check against the 1:6 ratio and credential validity.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
US$20–40/month per shop.

**How to find first customers:**
The BOT licensee list (if public, unverified) and DCLE course cohorts.

**Risks:**
Credentials renew yearly, so value is low. The feature could be added by existing dive booking software. The market is tiny.

**Kill condition:**
Fewer than 50 licensed operators, or dive booking software already tracks certifications. Many probably track instructor certs already (unverified).

**Willingness to pay / founder access:**
Low. Possible as a small regional add-on, alongside Guam, CNMI and Yap dive markets. A non-local could sell it remotely only with a partner in Koror.

**Score:** 2/10

## 3. Rejected

- **Scrap metal / junk-vehicle dealers.** There is no private dealer class. MPIIC's Division of Solid Waste Management runs collection and export, and scrap is worth about US$150/t (Island Times; SPREP J-PRISM country profile).
- **Taxi jeep licensing.** A 1963 Koror ordinance with a $20 fine, filed once a year at a municipal counter. No recurring workflow (kororstategov.com M.O. 24-62).
- **Pig farming and slaughter.** The BOA Animal Production Project supplies piglets and feed and buys the pigs back. The government holds the records (Island Times).
- **Inshore fish buyers.** BMR market data comes from research surveys, not mandatory dealer returns. The value chain is about US$3.2M (BMR 2014).
- **Food premises sanitation.** The Ministry of Health inspects. The operator only renews a permit, and I found no published form (25 PNC ch. 10).
- **Household employers of domestic workers.** Too few households, and they are already inside the general work-permit process.

## 4. Method notes

The regulator-first approach worked partly. Koror State's site (kororstategov.com) publishes ordinances and regulation PDFs, and Island Times and Marianas Variety are the best sources on enforcement. National registers (business licences, BOT licensees, work-permit holders) don't appear in search, so I could count no market from a register. Searches for livestock, food-safety and scrap mostly returned other jurisdictions or government-run programmes. In a microstate this size, the government is often the operator. English is the working language, so no local-language queries were needed.
