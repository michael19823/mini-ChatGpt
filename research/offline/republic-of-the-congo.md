# Republic of the Congo (Congo-Brazzaville): offline-industries pass

Research date: 2026-10-05. Treated as a **small, hard-to-reach market**: about 6 million people, French-speaking, XAF (CEMAC) currency, with most regulatory text published only as scanned PDFs in the Journal Officiel (sgg.cg). I made 13 web searches. Two more were refused for a usage limit, so I stopped and wrote up what I had. Many results were about the DR Congo (Kinshasa), Morocco or France, and I left those out.

**Bottom line:** none of the quiet industries I screened clears the bar. The regulator leaves very few traces online. Where an obligation does exist (moto-taxi registration, bureau de change licensing, CNSS for household employers), either the operators can't pay, or the market is a few dozen firms, or enforcement comes as one-off police sweeps rather than a recurring filing. Both opportunities below are kept as honest low scores, mainly as interview leads for a local founder. The country's only real software trigger is still SFEC / E-TAX / FOUTA, which is covered in the existing country report.

## 2. Opportunities (both weak)

### Opportunity: Compliance register and ID cards for moto-taxi associations

**Industry:**
Moto-taxi and tricycle passenger transport.

**Buyer:**
The drivers' secretariat or association ("secrétariat chargé du transport public par motocyclistes et tricycles"), a mayor's office, or a large motorbike fleet owner who rents bikes to drivers. Individual drivers are not realistic buyers.

**Trigger / Why now:**
In October 2025 the DGSP (presidential security directorate) gave moto-taxi drivers 72 hours to comply: registered bikes, approved helmets, **numbered fluorescent vests**, one passenger only, no foreign drivers. Training and equipment-kit distributions continued into 2026 (Makoua, Feb 2026). Someone now has to assign vest numbers, link each number to a driver, bike and registration, and show the police who is compliant.

**Current workflow:**
1. The association collects driver details and bike papers on paper.
2. It assigns vest numbers and hands out kits at physical events.
3. At roadside checks, the police inspect the bike papers and vest by eye.
4. Non-compliant drivers are stopped or their bikes impounded.

**Pain:**
Enforcement is real: there was a deadline and there are street checks. But the pain falls on drivers, who are the least able to pay. I found no evidence of how associations keep their lists today.

**Existing solutions:**
- Paper lists and registers kept by the association (assumed).
- The state vehicle registration system (cartes grises, plates).
- Ride-hailing and moto-taxi apps that operate in the region (not verified for Brazzaville).

**Offline evidence:**
Everything happens at roadside checks, training sessions and kit hand-outs. There is no portal or online register.

**Offline channel:**
The drivers' secretariat and the district kit distributions. A local founder would have to meet the secretariat in person. No public list exists to cold-call from.

**Market count:**
Unknown. There is no public register. Thousands of drivers is a plausible estimate, but there are only a handful of associations or municipalities to sell to.

**The gap:**
A simple register that links vest number, driver, bike plate and photo ID, with a QR code on the vest the police can scan to check compliance. It is unclear whether anyone would pay for this rather than a donor or the state.

**Possible product:**
A mobile register for associations: enrol a driver with a photo and papers, issue a numbered vest with a QR code, and give the police a public lookup page.

**MVP:**
An offline-capable Android form plus a printed QR card. Pilot with one association in one district.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
XAF 500–1,000 per driver per year collected through the association, or a one-off municipal contract. This is an estimate and it is very uncertain.

**How to find first customers:**
The secretariat named in the DGSP article, Brazzaville and Pointe-Noire city halls, and road-safety NGOs or donors who fund the kit distributions.

**Risks:**
- The buyer is political or security-led (the DGSP), so a foreign vendor is unlikely to be trusted.
- Revenue per driver is tiny.
- The state may build or contract this itself.

**Kill condition:**
If associations won't collect fees from drivers, or if the police won't use the lookup.

**Willingness to pay / founder access:**
Drivers would pay only for a done-for-you card bundled with the vest, and not for software. A non-local solo founder could not sell this: it needs a local partner with access to the DGSP and the town halls.

**Score:** 3/10

### Opportunity: Done-for-you CNSS and payslips for household employers (expatriate households)

**Industry:**
Households as employers of domestic workers: housekeepers, nannies, drivers, guards.

**Buyer:**
Expatriate households in Pointe-Noire and Brazzaville, often oil-sector staff, and the HR departments of oil companies or embassies that reimburse domestic staff costs.

**Trigger / Why now:**
The SMIG went up to XAF 70,400/month from 1 Jan 2025, and a new ITS income-tax scale applies from 2026 (see the country report). Employers must register with CNSS and pay monthly contributions.

**Current workflow:**
1. The household pays cash and keeps no payslip, or asks the company's HR or a local accountant to handle it.
2. CNSS registration and monthly payment are done at the counter, if at all.
3. On termination, severance is negotiated informally, which risks a dispute at the labour inspectorate.

**Pain:**
Mostly latent. Compliance looks low and I found no enforcement evidence for domestic workers. The pain only appears when a dismissed worker goes to the labour inspectorate.

**Existing solutions:**
- Local accountants and payroll bureaus.
- Congopaie and Sage Paie for companies.
- Corporate relocation and HR providers serving oil majors.
- Doing nothing, which is the dominant substitute.

**Offline evidence:**
No domestic-worker CNSS procedure is published online. Payment is at the counter or through an accountant.

**Offline channel:**
Oil-company HR and expatriate associations in Pointe-Noire, relocation agents, and embassies.

**Market count:**
Unknown. No statistic found. Probably a few thousand expatriate households (estimate), and shrinking with oil-sector headcount.

**The gap:**
Nobody packages "monthly payslip + CNSS + end-of-contract calculation" for one household with one or two staff.

**Possible product:**
A service with software behind it: monthly payslip, CNSS declaration filed by a local agent, and a severance calculator.

**MVP:**
A spreadsheet-backed payslip generator and severance calculator, plus a local accountant partner who files with CNSS.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
XAF 10,000–20,000 per household per month (estimate).

**How to find first customers:**
Oil-company HR, expatriate associations and relocation agents in Pointe-Noire.

**Risks:**
- Low enforcement means no urgency.
- Oil companies may already cover this through relocation providers.
- Needs a locally licensed accountant.

**Kill condition:**
If CNSS doesn't enforce against households, or if corporate HR already handles domestic staff for expatriates.

**Willingness to pay / founder access:**
Buyers would pay only for a done-for-you service, not for software. It needs a local accountant partner.

**Score:** 3/10

## 3. Rejected

- **Scrap metal dealers:** I found no dealer register or police reporting obligation. A few wholesalers, often foreign-owned, buy from informal street collectors. Source: https://fr.allafrica.com/stories/202408230436.html
- **Bureaux de change:** these are licensed one at a time by arrêté (e.g. Arrêté 1174/2025, San Money) under CEMAC Regulation 02/18. There are probably only tens of them, and they report to BEAC, which is closed to outside vendors. Sources: https://www.sgg.cg/textes-officiels/arretes/2025/congo-arrete-2025-1174.pdf, https://kalieu-elongo.com/reglementation-des-bureaux-de-change-en-zone-cemac-ce-quil-faut-savoir/
- **Gold buying counters / artisanal gold:** only permit arrêtés exist, I found no buyer register, and the AML risk is high. Source: https://www.sgg.cg/1/87/fr/recherche.html
- **Pharmacies and depots:** the problem is illicit trade (about 28% of urban supply), not a paperwork workflow. Source: https://issafrica.org/iss-today/political-promises-haven-t-solved-congo-brazzaville-s-illegal-medicine-trade
- **Urban minibuses:** the state passcard already covers e-payment, and operators are informal. Source: https://cio-mag.com/?p=19380
- **Fishers, abattoirs, funeral homes:** I found no Congo-specific obligation or register online, so there is no evidence to build on.

## 4. Method notes

- **What worked:** French news on allafrica.com (fr.allafrica.com) for enforcement campaigns, and sgg.cg gazette PDFs for individual licences.
- **What didn't work:** searchable licensing registers don't exist online. Queries about animals, funerals and fisheries returned Moroccan, Algerian, French or Swiss material. Many results were about the DR Congo.
- **What to do instead:** a local person would need to visit the town halls, CNSS and the drivers' secretariats in person.
- **Searches:** 13 completed and 2 refused for a usage limit, after which I stopped.
