# Sierra Leone: Offline (Quiet) Industries Pass (as of 2026-10-05)

> **Research constraints (read first).** This pass ran in two sessions. A WebSearch usage-limit
> refusal stopped the first session. The second resumed after the coordinator confirmed the limit
> had reset, and one more parallel call was refused partway through. In all, about 19 WebSearch
> calls were made against a budget of 20, two of them refused. WebFetch and GitHub tools were not used.
>
> **Verified** means the fact appeared in search-result content returned in this session (the
> underlying pages could not be opened). Rows marked *desk* come from background knowledge only
> and are **unverified**. No counts, names or URLs have been invented. Where a count is unknown,
> the report says so.
>
> Sierra Leone is a small, low-income and largely informal market. The first-round country report
> (`research/countries/sierra-leone.md`) found nothing above 3/10, and this pass does not change that.

**Bottom line.** The regulator-first method surfaced real, enforced obligations: Pharmacy Board
quarterly inspections and fines, okada/keke circulation permits, BSL forex-bureau licensing,
canoe registration, and NASSIT's coming informal-sector scheme. But none of these supports an
indie software business. Buyers are few or near-subsistence, enforcement is a flat fine of about
Le500, and the regulators either run their own channels or collect cash. The least-bad lead is
**inspection-readiness for pharmacy and drug-store outlets** at 3/10, with okada/keke fleet permits
at 2/10. Better treated as part of a regional West African product (Ghana, Liberia, Nigeria
pharmacy regulators) than as a standalone market.

## 2. Strongest opportunities

### Opportunity: Inspection-readiness pack for pharmacy and drug-store outlets

**Industry:**
Retail pharmacy, including drug stores and patent or chemical-seller outlets.

**Buyer:**
The owner or superintendent pharmacist of a small independent pharmacy or drug store,
especially owners of 2–5 outlets in Freetown, Bo or Makeni.

**Trigger / Why now:**
- The Pharmacy Board says its enforcement follows a **quarterly compliance framework** of routine
  inspections, hearings, and closure or revocation where needed.
- **August 2025:** 136 outlets sanctioned in Freetown.
- **October 2025:** 76 outlets fined in Bo and Makeni.
- Health leaders have publicly called current pharmacy laws "outdated and dangerous" and asked for
  reform. A change in the law could add new record obligations (unverified).

**Current workflow:** *(presumed; validate in interviews)*
1. Stock is bought from importers and wholesalers and logged in a paper stock book, or not at all.
2. A PBSL Distribution Chain inspector visits unannounced and checks the premises licence,
   whether qualified staff are present, whether the stock is registered and genuine, and how
   controlled drugs are stored.
3. Breaches are written up. The outlet is summoned to a disciplinary hearing and fined Le500 per
   breach, or closed.

**Pain:**
Fines and closures are verified. The direct fine is small (Le500 per breach is roughly $20–25,
*estimate* at the 2025 exchange rate). The real cost is lost trading days from a closure or seizure.
The violations that get sanctioned are mostly substantive (no pharmacist on site, counterfeit or
unregistered drugs), not record-keeping, which weakens the software angle.

**Existing solutions:**
- Paper stock books from local stationers (*desk*).
- Pharmacist-in-charge arrangements, where a pharmacist lends their name to a store (*desk*,
  unverified).
- Generic pharmacy POS and inventory software used by larger chains (*desk*, no Sierra Leone-specific
  product verified).
- The Pharmacy Board's own register of registered products, if published (unverified).
- The Pharmaceutical Society of Sierra Leone (*desk*).

**Offline evidence:**
Enforcement is by physical inspection and in-person hearings. Results are announced through
newspapers, not a portal. No local pharmacy-compliance software turned up in the search, and no
public premises list was found.

**Offline channel:**
Importers and wholesalers that supply these outlets; the Pharmaceutical Society (*desk*);
outlets named in PBSL hearing press releases. Contact by WhatsApp or phone, through a local
pharmacist partner.

**Market count:**
Unknown. At least 212 outlets were sanctioned at the two 2025 hearings, so the licensed base is
likely in the high hundreds to low thousands (*estimate*).

**The gap:**
A self-check against the registered-product list, a controlled-drug register, and a staff-roster
record kept ready to show an inspector. Whether the product list is available to software, and
whether inspectors accept a digital register, are both unverified.

**Possible product:**
A mobile stock book that flags products not on the PBSL register, keeps a Class A/B drugs ledger,
and produces a one-page "inspection file" per outlet.

**MVP:**
A WhatsApp-plus-web form to capture purchases and controlled-drug movements, a hand-kept list of
registered products, and a printable inspection summary.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
$5–15 per outlet per month (*estimate*), probably bundled with a wholesaler.

**How to find first customers:**
Outlets named in PBSL hearing press releases; a wholesaler partnership; a local pharmacist.
A non-local solo founder could not realistically sell this alone.

**Risks:**
Small fines give weak motivation; the main violations are not about software; PBSL may not
publish a machine-readable product register; very low price ceiling; a regional pharmacy
POS vendor could add this.

**Kill condition:**
Inspectors don't accept digital records, PBSL has no usable product list, or owners say a
fixer or a fine is cheaper than staying compliant.

**Score:** 3/10

### Opportunity: Permit-and-compliance tracker for okada/keke fleet owners

**Industry:**
Informal transport: commercial motorbikes (okada) and tricycles (keke).

**Buyer:**
An owner of several bikes or kekes who rents them to riders on a daily or weekly "work and pay"
basis, or a rider union branch. Individual riders are not realistic buyers.

**Trigger / Why now:**
- The Annual Circulation Permit under **section 11 of the Finance Act 2023** covers okadas and
  kekes. It is paid through slrsa.smartkorpor.com or a QR code.
- Freetown enforces **36 prohibited routes** (Mends Street was recently added). Violators face
  licence suspension. Police make periodic mass arrests (e.g. "Police arrests 70 Okadas").
- There is no new 2025–2026 trigger, which is a weakness.

**Current workflow:** *(presumed)*
1. The owner keeps papers for each bike in a folder or with the rider.
2. The permit is paid once a year online, by QR code or through an agent.
3. A bike stopped with lapsed papers is impounded, and the owner recovers it at a station or yard.

**Pain:**
Impoundment and fines (enforcement verified, costs unverified), plus lost daily rental income.

**Existing solutions:**
The SLRSA online/QR channel; agents and fixers (*desk*); rider unions (*desk*); WhatsApp reminders.

**Offline evidence:**
Enforcement is by roadside stop and arrest. No operator software was found.

**Offline channel:**
Park and union leaders; motorbike importers and dealers; SLRSA offices. Needs a local.

**Market count:**
Unknown. The SLRSA vehicle register would be the source.

**The gap:**
Expiry tracking and a per-bike document wallet. That is thin, and close to a generic reminder bot.

**Possible product:**
SMS or WhatsApp reminders plus document photos per bike, with an optional done-for-you renewal.

**MVP:**
A sheet of bikes with plate and expiry dates, SMS alerts 30/7/1 days before expiry, and document photos.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
Under $2 per bike per month, or a per-renewal agent fee (*estimate*). Only a done-for-you service
is plausible.

**How to find first customers:**
Union and park leaders in Freetown. A non-local founder can't sell this.

**Risks:**
Very low willingness to pay; SLRSA could add SMS reminders; CBD okada bans shrink the market.

**Kill condition:**
Agents already handle renewals cheaply, or impoundments are settled informally.

**Score:** 2/10

## 3. Rejected

- **Forex bureaus:** BSL licensing is real (application fees, NLe50,000–100,000 collateral, external auditors), but it is a one-off. No recurring return was verified, and bureaus are few. Sources: https://bsl.gov.sl/operating guidelines for foreign exchange bureaux.pdf ; https://awokonewspapersl.com/dollar-boys-present-documents-for-regularization/
- **Canoe registration and catch-based licensing:** 11,000+ canoes is a real count, but owners can't pay, and any registration or quota system would be a donor or government project. Sources: FAO tenure paper (fao.org/fishery/static/tenure-user-rights/root/volume6/C11...); https://awokonewspapersl.com/?p=147613
- **Scrap metal export:** the per-shipment Customs plus Ministry of Mines workflow involves only a handful of exporters, and clearing agents handle it.
- **NASSIT informal-sector scheme:** voluntary contributions on NASSIT-run rails; watch it, but there is no software buyer. Sources: https://awokonewspapersl.com/?p=155688 ; https://www.premiermedia-sl.com/?p=28290
- **Domestic workers, livestock, market traders, EPA small-business licences:** unenforced, no Sierra Leone evidence found, cash dues, or weak oversight, respectively.
- **Gold/diamond dealer ledgers:** already covered in the first-round report; MCAS is the substitute.

## 4. Method notes

- What worked: queries with a Sierra Leone agency name (Pharmacy Board, BSL, NASSIT, SLRSA, EPA-SL) plus "sanctioned", "fined" or "licence". Results came from local newspapers (awokonewspapersl.com, premiermedia-sl.com) and moice.gov.sl press releases, which act as the de facto enforcement record.
- What didn't: generic industry queries (livestock, scrap "ban/licence") return other countries' rules. No public licensee registers (pharmacy premises, forex bureaus) turned up, so market counts stay unknown.
- Two calls were refused for usage limits; about 19 of the 20 searches were used.
