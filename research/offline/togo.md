# Togo: offline / quiet-industries pass

Research date: 2026-10-05. Budget: 20 WebSearch calls. **8 were attempted; the 8th was refused ("usage limit")**, so the
instructions applied (stop and write up). In practice the report rests on 7 searches. WebFetch was not used.
Searches were in French. This is a short report on a small, mostly informal market. Most rows below are screened at
low confidence, and anything I could not check against a source is marked "unverified" or "estimate".

The existing country report (`research/countries/togo.md`) covered certified e-invoicing, CNSS/OTR payroll, EUDR
cocoa/coffee and customs brokers. None of those is repeated here.

## Context that shapes every quiet industry in Togo

- Most of the seed-list industries in Togo are **informal**. They do not hold a licence and do not keep a register. Where an obligation exists, the regulator is a ministry or a regional body
  (BCEAO, ECOWAS, CILSS/CSP), and the work goes through a counter or a ministry file, not an online portal.
- Public registers of licensed operators were **not findable online** for any industry I screened (scrap,
  pesticide distributors, forex bureaus, drillers). That makes the brief's "WhatsApp/phone outreach from a public
  register" channel weak almost everywhere.
- A non-local solo founder would need a local partner for almost all of these (French-speaking, cash/Mobile Money
  economy, relationship-based selling).

## 2. Opportunities

None reaches a level worth interviewing on its own. The two below are the least-bad candidates. I include them
so they can be compared across countries, and both are scored low on purpose.

### Opportunity: Done-for-you CNSS + IRPP compliance for household employers in Lomé

**Industry:**
Households as employers (domestic workers, nannies, drivers, guards).

**Buyer:**
Expatriate, diplomatic-staff, NGO-staff and upper-middle-class Togolese households in Lomé that employ 1 to 4
domestic staff. The secondary buyer is an NGO or embassy HR desk that wants its staff's households to be compliant.

**Trigger / Why now:**
Weak. Universal health insurance (AMU) contributions were added to CNSS in January 2024, and CNSS has moved monthly
declarations online (see country report). I found no 2025-26 rule aimed specifically at households.

**Current workflow:**
1. In most cases the employer does nothing: the worker is undeclared (estimate, unverified).
2. A compliant household registers the worker at a CNSS counter within 8 days of hire.
3. Each month the household computes pay, CNSS/AMU and IRPP, and declares and pays by the 15th. In practice an accountant or the employer's company HR often does this by hand.
4. At year end it files the annual summary to the OTR and CNSS.

**Pain:**
Low. Penalties exist but are rarely enforced on households. The real pain shows up at severance or in a
dispute, when unpaid contributions come to light. I found no source on enforcement.

**Existing solutions:**
- Doing nothing (the main substitute).
- Local accountants (cabinets comptables) charging a small monthly fee (unverified).
- Employer HR or relocation services for expatriates.
- CNSS e-services and its DRC Excel template ([CNSS guide](https://cnss.tg/cnss-media/2024/01/GUIDE-EXCEL_DRC.pdf)).

**Offline evidence:**
Registration and disputes go through CNSS counters and the labour inspectorate. I found no household-employer
product or page for Togo.

**Offline channel:**
Embassy and NGO HR desks, expat associations and international schools in Lomé. A local partner is required.

**Market count:**
Unknown. I found no CNSS statistic on household employers. Estimate: low thousands of plausible paying households.

**The gap:**
A household with one nanny has no simple way to stay compliant monthly, and accountants are oversized for the job.

**Possible product:**
A done-for-you service with a thin software layer: a monthly payslip and contribution calculator, plus a local agent who files and pays.

**MVP:**
A WhatsApp intake form, a payslip PDF and contribution amounts, filed by a local agent through CNSS e-services.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
5,000 to 10,000 FCFA (about $8 to $17) per household per month (estimate).

**How to find first customers:**
Embassy, UN and NGO HR focal points in Lomé; expat community groups.

**Risks:**
Tiny market; a service business, not software; needs a local operator; little enforcement means little urgency.

**Kill condition:**
Interviews show expat employers already have this done through their employer's HR or accountant, or don't declare at all.

**Score:** 2/10. Willingness to pay exists only for a service, not for software. A non-local founder cannot sell it.

**Sources:**
- https://rivermate.com/fr/guides/togo/impots
- https://cnss.tg/cnss-media/2024/01/GUIDE-EXCEL_DRC.pdf
- https://cnss.tg/cnss-media/2023/03/CAISSE-NATIONALE-DE-SECURITE-SOCIALE-DU-TOGO_DEFINITIF-1.pdf

### Opportunity: Approved-product checker and sales register for agro-input dealers

**Industry:**
Pesticide distributors and agro-dealers (rural input shops).

**Buyer:**
Owners of approved pesticide distribution businesses and their village-level resellers. A plausible alternative buyer
is an input-supply programme (donor or cotton company) that wants traceable sales.

**Trigger / Why now:**
Weak. The registration regime (national CPP list plus the regional CSP list, 172 products) is long-standing. NGO reports
on highly hazardous pesticides (IPEN; 16 HHPs, about 27.5% of the approved list) keep up pressure for stricter
control. I found no 2025-26 rule that adds a register or reporting duty.

**Current workflow:**
1. The distributor obtains approval (500,000 FCFA synthetic, 250,000 FCFA bio).
2. Products are sold over the counter, often on credit. Whether a sales register is kept is unverified.
3. An inspection, if any, checks products against the approved list on paper.

**Pain:**
Low to moderate and mostly reputational or donor-driven. The fake and unregistered-pesticide problem is real across West
Africa, but I did not verify Togo enforcement campaigns.

**Existing solutions:**
- The paper CPP/CSP list.
- Dealer networks run by cotton companies or donor input programmes.
- Generic stock apps.

**Offline evidence:**
No public online list of approved distributors or products found; approvals are handled through the ministry counter.

**Offline channel:**
The cotton sector and agricultural cooperative networks, and NGO input programmes (for example the AVSF/RENAAT network on
pesticides). A local partner is needed.

**Market count:**
Unknown; no public register found.

**The gap:**
Dealers have no easy way to check that a product is on the approved list, or to show a buyer or inspector a sales record.

**Possible product:**
A phone tool that checks a product label against the CPP/CSP list and keeps a simple sales register.

**MVP:**
A searchable approved-product list plus a basic sales log, exported to PDF.

**Pricing hypothesis:**
Paid by a programme or input company per dealer, around $2 to $5 per month (estimate). Dealers themselves are unlikely to pay.

**How to find first customers:**
Agricultural ministry plant protection directorate, cotton company input networks, NGO programmes.

**Risks:**
No recurring legal duty; donor-funded procurement; a regional CSP tool could cover it.

**Kill condition:**
No legal sales-register duty exists, or the CSP or a donor already distributes a free checker.

**Score:** 2/10.

- https://ipen.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/hhpsTogo2021_EN_executive_summary-en.pdf

## 3. Rejected

- **Scrap-metal dealer compliance:** The 2021 export ban and the 2024 localisation rule concentrate trade in a few local
  recyclers. I found no dealer register or police reporting duty.
- **Livestock / transhumance certificates (CIT, PGT 2026-2030):** A real 2026 policy trigger, but the buyer is the state or a donor.
  Herders and traders will not pay, and selling would mean government procurement.
- **Moto-taxi professionalisation:** A real 2025 plan with IFC support. The buyers are the state, unions or lenders, and
  it depends on informal drivers changing behaviour.
- **Well drillers:** Few firms, reporting is a contract deliverable to one client, and work is won through public tenders.
- **Money changers:** Few formal bureaus; the informal cambistes stay outside the system.

## 4. Method notes

- French regulator-first queries did surface the obligations (GTA for trade measures, IPEN/NGO reports for pesticides,
  Ecofin and allAfrica for policy plans). They did **not** surface any public licensing register or operator list.
  Togo does not publish them online, so the market could not be counted for any industry.
- Seed-list items tied to police registers (second-hand, pawn, gold) produced nothing. Togo has no such regime visible online.
- Search budget ended early because of a usage-limit refusal after 7 successful searches. Driving schools, abattoirs and funeral
  services were not screened.
- Overall: Togo's quiet industries are informal, and their "regulator" is often a donor-backed ministry programme. Togo is a poor
  market for this pass. Any product here would need a local operator and would be a service first.
